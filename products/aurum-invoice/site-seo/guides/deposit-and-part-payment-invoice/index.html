Deposits and part payments: how to invoice them

Updated 12 November 2026 · 2 min read

Deposits protect your cash flow, and part payments help customers afford larger purchases. The key is to keep one invoice as the single source of truth, and track every payment against it.

Why take a deposit

  • It proves the customer is serious.
  • It covers your first costs, like materials or booking a slot.
  • It reduces no-shows and last-minute cancellations.

For custom work, 30% to 50% upfront is common. Say so on your quotation, so it is expected from the start.

The simple way to track it

  1. Issue one invoice for the full amount.
  2. When the deposit arrives, issue a receipt for the deposit and record it against the invoice.
  3. Show total, paid so far and balance due on the invoice.
  4. When the balance arrives, issue a final receipt and mark the invoice paid.

Write the rules down

Deposit termsA 50% deposit is required to confirm your booking. The balance is due 3 days before the event. The deposit is non-refundable if you cancel within 7 days of the event.

Adjust the percentages and dates to suit your business, and make sure your refund rule is something you can honour.

Common mistakes

  • Taking money with no receipt. Always issue one.
  • Making a new unrelated invoice for the balance. Keep one invoice and show payments against it.
  • Not stating the refund rule up front.

Questions people ask

How much deposit should I ask for?

Thirty to fifty percent is common for custom work. Larger or riskier jobs may need more.

Do I issue a receipt for a deposit?

Yes. Issue a receipt for every payment, and show the balance still owed.

Can I refund a deposit?

That is up to your terms. State your rule clearly before the customer pays.
