Purchase orders explained for small businesses

Updated 12 November 2026 · 2 min read

A purchase order (PO) is a document a buyer sends to a seller to order goods or services. If you supply companies, you will start receiving them. If you buy in bulk, you should start sending them.

What a purchase order does

A PO states exactly what the buyer wants, at what price and by when. Once the seller accepts, it acts as a record that both sides agreed the order. Later, the seller's invoice should match the PO.

Purchase order vs invoice

  • Purchase order: sent by the buyer before the sale, to place the order.
  • Invoice: sent by the seller after, to request payment.

Many companies will not pay an invoice unless it quotes a valid PO number.

What to include

  • The words Purchase Order and a PO number.
  • The buyer's and seller's details.
  • Each item, quantity and agreed price.
  • Delivery address and date.
  • Payment terms, such as "30 days after invoice".
  • An authorised name or signature.

If you receive a PO

Check the items and prices match your quotation, then confirm the order in writing. Put the PO number on your invoice, so the buyer's accounts team can match it and pay faster.

If you send a PO

Number your POs in order, keep a copy, and check the supplier's invoice against it before you pay. It stops overcharging and gives you a clear paper trail.

Questions people ask

Who issues a purchase order, the buyer or the seller?

The buyer. The seller replies with an invoice.

Do I need a purchase order for a small purchase?

Not always, but it is good practice for larger or repeat orders, and companies often require them.

Why do companies ask for a PO number on invoices?

So their accounts team can match your invoice to an approved order before paying.
